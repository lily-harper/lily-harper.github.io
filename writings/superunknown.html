Project reflection

Superunknown

The gap between real-world plausibility and predictive value.

August 2026 View related project

“If this isn’t what you see
It doesn’t make you blind”
Soundgarden, Superunknown

Why

My goal was to take a messy, real-world dataset, with information entered by real observers, and feed it into a predictive model. Between cleaning the data and fitting the model, I wanted to reconstruct something closer to the crashes themselves than the original columns gave. Feature engineering became a way to assemble the recorded attributes, such as directions of travel, locations, times, road conditions, and driver actions, into a richer representation of each event.

I did not want to treat every column as an isolated fact. I thought that combining them could represent the relationships between them. A crash on a highway at night could have a different result than one during bumper-to-bumper traffic, all else equal.

I assumed that a feature resembling the physical event would be useful for prediction, but this project showed me that there are different standards that work in tandem.

Reconstructing What Wasn’t Observed

Neither my model nor I had observed the crashes directly. It was only a hypothesis that modifying speed limits with rush hour would be more predictive than rush hour or speed limit alone.

My approximation of the highway interaction did not have the predictive improvement I had hoped for. It could mean the theory was wrong, or that the available variables were too crude to represent the mechanism well. The speed limit of the roadway, taken from another dataset, served as a proxy for operating speed, but other physical features, such as lane position or vehicle mass, were unknown. Thus, a variable designed to separate two different types of highway crashes might instead be separating events with similar kinematics.

Conclusion

This exposed a potential tension between features justified by real-world plausibility and those justified by demonstrated predictive value. The model could gain no information from a feature that I thought mattered. On the other hand, predictive power can establish usefulness without simultaneously establishing meaning in the mechanisms. I do want to avoid overstating the divide, though.

I now think of that gap as the underlying superunknown, which could include information not recorded, reasons why a mechanism was misrepresented, or patterns that exist without a satisfying explanation.

This tension changed how I viewed data wrangling and modeling. I started the project wanting to reconstruct the crash in more detail by encoding assumptions about how its recorded attributes interacted. I finished the project with a stricter standard: the predictive power a feature has is not predicated on its persuasive concept. The story behind a feature can justify creating and testing it, but its performance, judged on an objective criterion in a model applied to unseen data, is what provides the justification for relying on it for prediction. When the feature fails, what is unknown is whether I misunderstood the crash or simply failed to represent it.
