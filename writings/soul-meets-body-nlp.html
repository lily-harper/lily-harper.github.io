Project reflection

Can Soul Meets Body meet NLP?

I wanted to analyze some songs I like, so I turned them into rows, vectors, and put them into clusters, but I gained more understanding from what the clusters didn’t show.

June 2026 View related project

"I cursed myself for being surprised
that this didn't play like it did in my mind"
- Death Cab for Cutie (Bixby Canyon Bridge).

Why

This project was conceived from a desire to learn how to implement natural language processing (NLP) and unsupervised machine learning (ML) techniques in a domain I like.

One of my favorite musicians of all time is Benjamin Gibbard of Death Cab for Cutie and the Postal Service. His unique vocals and musicianship, especially on acoustic arrangements, contribute to this, but what stands out to me is the earnest symbolism in his lyrics. I wanted to use data science to find underlying patterns that are less prominent through listening alone.

What I Did

After compiling and wrangling all the lyrics written and published by Gibbard, I employed popular mathematical techniques to transform the words into quantitative representations. I applied Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER) to get sentiment scores. Each song was represented by a vector using Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF), which weighs terms based on their frequencies relative to the corpus. I then reduced the dimensionality of that matrix using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and, with KMeans, tried to find song clusters within it.

What I Found

The results were less revelatory than I had hoped, but they were useful in another way.

  • Estimated sentiment differed between the Postal Service album and the Death Cab for Cutie albums released around the same time. It also changed across Death Cab’s discography, which spans more years than I’ve been alive.
  • One cluster contained almost every song from the most recent album, suggesting that its lyrics are especially cohesive.
  • Even without strong clusters, I learned a lot about completing a project and applying basic NLP and unsupervised ML methods—and I heard some songs I hadn’t encountered before.

The most important thing I learned was that sometimes what is lost in the reduction and computation is more interesting than what can be found. Not everything can be discretized and cleanly categorized into a single, mathematically formed cluster. Maybe the limitations reveal the parts of a song that really matter.

What the Methods Missed

The largest and most glaring limitation in analyzing songs using NLP is that I lost the insight that would be gleaned from the musicality of the song itself and the interaction between the music and words. NLP only attempts to handle the words themselves—not the notes, dynamics, keys, time signatures, or performances. I had to calibrate my expectations early and remind myself that I was analyzing the lyrics in the songs, not the songs themselves.

Another limitation can be found in the method I used for sentiment analysis. I chose VADER because it is useful for social media analysis and can handle punctuation, intensifiers, and even emojis. However, it ignores the context of words and cannot detect sarcasm or irony. One example is the neutral VADER score returned for a lyric from “Tiny Vessels” on Transatlanticism: “that you told her that you love her but you don’t.”

Another limitation in the process is the violation of one of the assumptions in the machine-learning method I used. KMeans assumes that each observation—in this case, each song—belongs to only one cluster. However, Ben Gibbard writes songs that belong to multiple themes at once. This is one reason why the evaluation metrics indicated weak clustering, including high values for the Davies-Bouldin Index and low Silhouette Scores.

Conclusion

As I learn more ML and NLP, I could integrate more techniques, including other clustering algorithms, a topic-modeling process, or transformer-based models. I could even add more data, such as key, time signatures, or album metadata. However, I think listening to the discographies front to back taught me more about why one of my favorite bands holds that space for me and many others.

Turning poetry and art into vectors was useful because it allowed me to compare metrics across a couple hundred songs, but it also stripped from each song what, in my opinion, makes a song special. I could not add ambiguity, personal association, and musical context into the matrix. I realized that I did not take these things away; rather, I added a matrix on the side—not to replace the songs, but to supplement them.
